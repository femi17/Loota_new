The Bronze Compass

A Loota treasure hunt in five keys

Everything you need is in this story. Five places are hidden in it, somewhere in the world. Read it closely, keep your radio near you, and trust no one with a soft voice.

Prologue: The Night of the Leopard

Benin City · February 1897

The night the soldiers came, Ehizogie was still at his furnace.

All evening the drums had been wrong. Not the drums of a festival or a funeral, but quick, broken ones, passed from compound to compound like a rumour. By the time the moon was up, the air over the casters' street had turned orange, and somewhere beyond the great red-earth walls the guns had begun their flat, stupid coughing.

Ehizogie did not hurry. Forty years at the furnace had taught him that bronze punishes a hurried man. He waited until the crucible glowed the colour of a ripe palm fruit, poured, counted under his breath, and only then broke the clay mould open with the back of his hammer.

Inside lay a compass no bigger than a child's palm. Its rim was a ring of tiny leopards walking nose to tail. Its needle was a single thin blade of brass. And where every other compass in the world says north, Ehizogie had carved one word in the old way of writing his grandfather had taught him: home.

His daughter Osayi was eleven. She stood in the doorway with her hands pressed over her ears, because the guns were louder now and the dogs of the whole quarter were screaming.

"Papa," she said, "they are taking everything. The plaques from the king's pillars. The heads from the altars. Mama says they are putting them in boxes for the sea. If they take everything, where is home?"

Ehizogie knelt so that his face was level with hers. He put the warm compass in her hands, closed her fingers over it, and held them there.

"Home," he said, "is where the leopard sits on the king's wall. Say it."

"Home is where the leopard sits on the king's wall."

"Good. Whatever they take, the compass will remember it. One day it will find its way back, and when it does, you will know where to stand."

Then, because he knew the soldiers would search the children too, he gently took it back.

Before dawn, Ehizogie packed the compass in straw at the bottom of a crate of bronze plaques that the soldiers had already marked for the ships. He scratched three letters onto thin sheets of brass, the kind casters use to test their alloy, and on each he pressed his mark: a tiny leopard, sitting, with its tail curled to the left. "A true leopard always looks back towards the king," he used to say, "and the king sits on the left hand of the morning."

Where he hid the letters, nobody in the family ever knew. The crates went down the creeks to the sea. The ships sailed. Osayi grew up, and grew old, and told the story of the compass to her son, who told it to his son, who became a professor and told it to his granddaughter, who mostly thought it was a lovely story.

Osayi died still waiting for a letter. She never found one.

1. The Fridge

Lagos · today

More than a century later, Isoken Osagie let herself into her grandfather's flat at ten past five on a Thursday, and knew before she had shut the door that something was wrong.

It was the smell first: not the usual smell of old paper, camphor and the pepper soup he swore was medicine, but the dusty, opened-up smell of a place that has been searched. Every drawer in the sitting room was pulled out. Every book was on the floor, spines cracked, as if someone had shaken each one upside down to see what fell out. The cushions of his good chair had been slit and gutted.

"Grandpa?"

Nothing. The bedroom was the same. So was the little study where Professor Osagie, retired curator, retired lecturer, retired from almost everything except arguing, had spent forty years writing a book about Benin bronzes that he had never finished and never would.

Only one thing in the whole flat was untouched: the small black internet radio on the windowsill. He had bought it three years ago, when his knees stopped letting him make the long drive home to Benin City, and every single evening since, it had brought home to him across four hundred kilometres: Radio Benin, ninety-seven point three, "the only station that still reads the news properly". Its little green light was on. Whoever had searched the flat had not thought a radio worth searching.

Isoken stood in the middle of the wreckage and made herself breathe. She thought about the police and almost laughed. She thought about her aunt, who lived off Siluko Road in Benin City and who would scream down the phone and be no use at all. She thought about the strange man who had come to the archive last month asking, very politely, whether Professor Osagie still lived at this address.

Then she saw the fridge.

Under the magnet shaped like a leopard, the one she had bought him at a craft market when she was nine, was a sheet of yellow paper in his careful, slanting hand.

Isoken. They came for the compass. I have gone back to where it began, before they find it.

Six o'clock news. Between the weather and the football, I'll tell you where to find me.

Trust no one with a soft voice.

And remember what I taught you on the way home from church.

She read it four times. The compass. So it was real. Of course it was real. Grandpa had never told her a story that wasn't, in some infuriating way, true.

Remember what I taught you on the way home from church. That one made her eyes sting. When she was small and her parents were abroad, she had spent every Sunday with him, and the long walk home from the cathedral had been a game. When the bells rang the hour, she had to count them out loud. "However many bells," he would say, "that many steps. And then you walk backwards." She would shriek with laughter, stumbling backwards along the pavement while market women shook their heads at the mad old professor and his mad little girl. She had always thought it was nonsense. He had always been perfectly serious.

She looked at her watch. Twelve minutes past five. The six o'clock news was in forty-eight minutes.

The radio on the windowsill gave a small electronic chirp, the sound it made when it lost the internet and found it again. Isoken looked at it. Then at the street below, where a dark car she did not recognise had been parked when she arrived and was still parked now, engine running.

She put the note in her bra, picked up her bag, and left by the back stairs.

2. The Soft Voice

Lagos · evening

The first taxi she flagged on the main road was a clean, old silver car with a bead curtain hanging from the mirror. The driver was a big man in a white shirt, and when he spoke his voice was surprisingly gentle, like a pastor at a child's funeral.

"Where to, madam?"

"Just drive, please. Towards the bridge. I'll tell you." She hesitated, then leaned forward. "And can you put the radio on? Ninety-seven point three. Radio Benin. They stream it."

He smiled at her in the mirror. "Radio Benin, in Lagos? You must be homesick, madam." But he found it on his phone and plugged it into the dashboard, and the car filled with the dry, tidy voice of a man who had been reading the news for thirty years.

The six o'clock pips came. Isoken realised she was gripping the seat in front of her.

The headlines rolled past: a quarrel in the state assembly about a new bridge; a ferry held up by fog on the Ikpoba river; the price of petrol, again; a famous musician's wedding. Then the newsreader cleared his throat, the way he always did before the weather, and said, "And now, the weather. A hot and humid evening across the city, with thunderstorms expected later over…"

The taxi rolled up onto the long concrete bridge across the lagoon, and dipped under the overhead gantry, and Isoken saw the driver's hand drift, slowly, to the phone on the dashboard.

The newsreader's voice broke into a hiss, then into nothing.

"Signal," said the driver softly. "It is always the bridge."

When the voice came back, a sports reporter was shouting about a penalty in the eighty-ninth minute. Whatever had come between the weather and the football was gone.

Isoken sat very still. The driver's phone buzzed on the dashboard. He picked it up without looking at her, listened, and said in that gentle voice, "Yes. The seven o'clock. It's done." Then he hung up, and smiled at her again in the mirror, and asked whether madam would like the air conditioning higher.

Trust no one with a soft voice.

At the foot of the bridge the traffic thickened, the way Lagos traffic does, out of nowhere, as if the city had suddenly remembered it had somewhere to be. When the lights at the junction turned red and the taxi stopped behind a yellow bus, Isoken opened the door and ran.

She heard him call after her, still gently. She did not look back.

She caught the night bus to Benin City with forty minutes to spare and the last seat at the back, next to a woman with a basket of smoked fish and a boy asleep on her shoulder.

At seven o'clock, the driver turned his radio up loud for the news. It was Radio Benin too. The whole bus listened: the bridge quarrel, the fog, the petrol, the wedding, the weather, and then a notice, read in the same dry voice, telling the owner of something lost exactly where to collect it. A man across the aisle laughed at it. Isoken turned her face to the window and did not listen. The seven o'clock. It's done.

Instead she borrowed the boy's mother's phone, found the station's page, and pressed the little button that said Listen again. She chose the six o'clock bulletin. She put the phone against her ear, closed her eyes, and listened to every word: the headlines, the weather, and then, at last, the part between the weather and the football that the bridge and the soft-voiced man had stolen from her.

She listened to it three times. Then she gave back the phone, said thank you, and slept for the first time that day.

By sunrise the next morning, she was standing exactly where the six o'clock news had told her to go.

3. Five Messages

Benin City · sunrise

The street smelled of charcoal and hot metal, and the morning was already full of hammers.

Isoken had been here as a child, on one of Grandpa's long weekends home, when he had bought her roasted corn on Oba Market Road and complained about the traffic on Akpakpava Road and taken her to his favourite chop bar on Mission Road, where the owner called him Prof and gave him extra meat. She remembered the smoke and the shining heads and leopards for sale on every doorstep. She did not remember it being so quiet underneath the noise, as if every caster on the street were listening.

In a workshop with a leopard painted over its door, an old man was already pumping his bellows. He did not look up.

"You are the Professor's girl," he said. "He said you would come. He also said you would be late."

His name was Pa Eweka, and his family had poured bronze on this street for longer than anyone could count. He wiped his hands on a rag that had been white once and took an old grey mobile phone out of a biscuit tin.

"Your grandfather sat in that chair for three days," he said, "and on the fourth day he went. Where, he would not tell me. 'If you don't know, Eweka, they cannot make you say.' But every night since, he has sent messages to this phone. Riddles. He says the masked man's people listen to everything I say, so he talks to me in nonsense." He snorted. "Five messages. All nonsense. Listen, and you tell me which is for you."

He pressed play.

The first message came from somewhere with wind in it.

"Eweka, my friend. Pack light, if anyone asks you to travel. Only the leopard matters now. Remember the bells. Take the early flight, never the late one. Old friends will help you."

The second was shorter, and sounded tired.

"Eweka. Rest tonight. Order nothing to the house, not even food. Move the brass at dawn. Ask your sons to watch the road."

The third had a crowd behind it, and a church bell.

"Eweka, listen. Ask no questions of strangers. Call nobody from your own phone. Carry the leopard only in daylight. Rest when you can. Always look behind you."

The fourth was almost a whisper.

"Eweka, I am sorry for all this. Lock the workshop at night. Answer no calls you don't know. Go nowhere alone. Only trust family. Sleep with the light on."

And the fifth was different. He sounded closer, and almost cheerful, the way he sounded when he had won an argument.

"Bring the leopard to me. Every hour counts now. Listen for the bells. Eat before you fly. Mind the masked man. This one is for Isoken."

Pa Eweka shook his head. "You see? Nonsense, five times. 'Bring the leopard', 'carry the leopard', 'only the leopard matters'. What leopard? I have a hundred leopards." He nodded at the shelves.

But he reached under the bench all the same and brought out a small brass leopard, sitting, with its tail curled to the left. "This one he left with me. He said: the girl will know it."

Isoken knew it. It was the mark from the story, the one Ehizogie pressed on his letters: a leopard sitting, looking back towards the king, its tail curled to the left.

Outside, a car door slammed.

Through the gap in the workshop wall Isoken saw a black car that had not been there a minute ago, two men in dark shirts getting out of it, and between them a figure in a fine grey suit whose face was a carved wooden mask: a long, pale, smiling face with slits for eyes.

"Back door," said Pa Eweka, very calmly, already moving. "Go through my neighbour's yard. Go."

As she ducked under the back lintel, she saw the masked man pause on the step to shake something out of his trouser leg: three small paper tags, the kind airlines tie to suitcases. They fluttered onto the step. Pa Eweka swept them up with his foot as he passed, pressed them into her hand at the back door, and pushed her out into the yard.

"He came in from the city of the seven hills," the old man whispered. "Everybody on this street knows it; he boasts about it. That is where he goes back to. Be careful, Professor's girl."

She ran through the neighbour's yard, past a startled goat and a line of washing, out into a lane, and kept running until the hammers were far behind her. Only on the bus to the airport did she open her hand and look at the tags. Three of them, each crumpled and stained, each printed with three black letters: FCO, IST, EBB.

She thought about cities of seven hills. She remembered a university friend from Kampala who used to boast that her city sat on seven hills, just like Rome. She remembered a schoolteacher who had said the same about Istanbul. She remembered Grandpa, laughing, insisting that Yaoundé had seven hills too and nobody ever gave it the credit.

Then she thought about five messages, and which one had been for her, and what it had really said.

She bought a plane ticket with the last of her savings. This story will not tell you where she flew. Only that she ate before she flew, because her grandfather had told her to.

4. Eleven Bells

Somewhere by a river

The river was so wide that at first she thought it was the sea. It was silver in the morning light, and the wind coming off it smelled of salt and of sardines grilling somewhere, and the trams that rattled along the waterfront were yellow and so old they looked like toys.

On the far bank, high on a hill, a giant stone figure stood with its arms spread wide over the water, as if blessing the ships, or warning them. Isoken stared at it. She had seen a figure like that before, on a postcard from the other side of the ocean, a famous one on a mountain above a famous bay. It was not the same. She told herself it was not the same.

Grandpa had always said there was a place "where the sailors touched the stones for luck before they left, and came back to touch them again, if they came back at all". She walked the waterfront slowly, the brass leopard heavy in her pocket, looking at stones.

Behind her, the bells of a great old monastery began to ring the hour. She counted them without meaning to, the way a child of the church walk always would.

Eleven.

She found the first strip of brass where the old stones by the water were worn smooth by hands. It was wedged in a crack so neatly that it might have grown there. Scratched on it were thirteen letters, and in the corner, very small, a leopard sitting with its tail curled to the right.

X L I   L S V R M Q E R

Under it, newer, in pencil: Meet me at the Tower. M.

"M," she breathed. The Masquerade had been here first.

She almost left. Then she remembered how neatly Grandpa did everything, and walked on along the water, touching stone after stone, until her fingers found a second crack and a second strip, dull and old and real. Thirteen letters again, and in the corner a leopard, sitting, with its tail curled to the left.

R C P L E   C F D D P W W

She had just slipped it into her pocket when a hand closed on her shoulder. She twisted, felt a sleeve tear, and ran: along the river, between tourists and fishermen, across the tram lines in front of a clanging yellow tram that hid her for three precious seconds. When she looked back, a man in a grey suit was standing on the far side of the tracks, very still, holding the wooden mask loosely at his side like a hat. He did not chase her. He just watched.

That night, in a hostel bunk that smelled of other people's sunscreen, Isoken laid both strips of brass on her knees and stared at them until the letters swam.

To keep from panicking, she read the news on her phone. There was a long article about museums in Europe giving bronzes back to Benin. A college in Cambridge had returned a bronze cockerel that had stood in its dining hall for a century. A small museum in south London had handed over seventy-two objects at once. A university in Aberdeen had sent home a bronze head, and a city in Scotland had promised to do the same. Every museum in the article sounded like a place a brass letter might be waiting. She closed the article before it could make her any more confused.

She tried the strip with the right-curled leopard first, because it had a message on it, and messages are easier than nonsense. She tried shifting its letters forwards and backwards, one step, two steps, eleven steps. Eleven steps backwards gave her MAX AHKGBFTG: nonsense. She stopped, and looked again at the little leopard in the corner, its tail curled the wrong way, and felt cold.

A true leopard always looks back towards the king.

She put that strip face down on the blanket and did not look at it again.

Then she took the real one, the one with its leopard curled to the left, and she heard Grandpa's voice as clearly as if he were sitting on the end of the bunk: However many bells, that many steps. And then you walk backwards.

She had counted eleven bells.

She walked every letter eleven steps backwards through the alphabet, one by one, in pencil, on the back of her boarding pass. The first word came out at two in the morning. The second at a quarter to three. By the time the birds started outside the hostel window, she knew which city she had to go to next, and which street, and she was laughing and crying at the same time, because it was exactly the kind of thing Grandpa would think was funny.

5. The Grey City

A cold November morning

The grey city was waking slowly under a sky the colour of a used teabag. Isoken had been told all her life that it rained here every day. It did not rain. It just sulked.

The museum on the street from the brass strip was so big that she walked around two sides of it before she found the way in. Inside, a roof of glass made of a thousand triangles hung over a great white courtyard, and under it the morning light came down soft and even, like flour through a sieve. She stood under it for a full minute before she remembered why she had come.

The gallery of her city's bronzes was at the back. She found it by the leopards.

She stood in front of a bronze leopard for an hour. She did not know what she was looking for. She only knew that Grandpa had stood here, years ago, on the one trip abroad he never stopped talking about, and that the strip of brass had sent her to this street and not another.

"You've got the look."

The guard was a small woman in her seventies with silver hair cut short and a uniform that was slightly too big for her. Her badge said MARGARET.

"Benin," she said. "I can always tell. You stand in front of them like you're visiting a relative in hospital. My grandmother used to stand in front of her own country's things like that, in other people's museums." She smiled. "Forty-one years I've been looking after old things, love. You learn the look."

They talked, because the gallery was empty and Isoken had not talked to a kind person in three days. Margaret had worked in museums "all over the shop". She had started as a girl in Vienna, "in the old emperors' palace, where they keep half the world in glass cases and the other half in the basement", and moved to Copenhagen for a man, "who was a mistake, but the palace was lovely. It's on its own little island, and they've built it three times now, because it keeps burning down."

She had spent a winter in Stockholm, "where the king's palace sits right next to the old church on its island, and the museum looks at it across the water, and it's so cold your thoughts freeze halfway out". She had spent a terrible, wonderful summer in Madrid, "where the palace and the cathedral are so close you could pass a cup of tea from one window to the other".

"Half the bronzes aren't even here, you know," she said, nodding at the cases. "People think it's all here. It isn't. They went everywhere. Paris has some, of course: in the old king's palace on the river, the one with the glass pyramid in its courtyard, full to the ceiling with the world. Prague I don't know about, though I loved Prague: a castle with a whole cathedral inside its walls, imagine. Budapest, the palace on the hill above the river. Warsaw, where they built the royal castle again brick by brick after the war, from old paintings, if you can believe it.

"And Berlin," she said. "Berlin has lots of yours. Their king's palace was knocked down after the war, flat, gone, and only a few years ago they built the whole thing all over again, on the island in their river, right next to their big cathedral, looking across the gardens at all the museums. No king has slept in it for a hundred years. Now it's full of the world's treasures. Ugly on one side and pretty on the other, like most of my husbands."

Isoken laughed for the first time in days. And at that moment, at exactly nine o'clock by the gallery clock, her phone rang.

A number she did not know. A voice she did not know: smooth, amused, beautifully polite.

"Good morning, little historian. I hope you slept. Here it is already ten, and the cathedral bells next door are giving me a terrible headache. I am standing in the king's house. Not the old one: the one they built twice, where no king has slept for a century. I am looking across the gardens at the museums, and I am holding your great-great-great-grandfather's last letter in my hand. It is very beautiful. Come and take it from me, if you can."

The line went dead.

Margaret was watching her. "Who was that, love?"

Isoken looked at the gallery clock, which said one minute past nine. She looked at the phone, which said the call had come from a foreign number. She thought about everything Margaret had just told her, about palaces and islands and cathedrals and kings, and about which of them could be an hour ahead of this grey, sulking city on a November morning, and built twice, and next to a cathedral, and looking across gardens at museums, and empty of kings for a hundred years.

"Someone with a soft voice," she said.

6. The King's House

Closing time

She found him in an empty gallery at closing time, standing in front of a long glass case of bronzes from her city, exactly as if he were waiting for a bus.

The wooden mask was in his hand. Without it he was just an old man in an expensive grey coat, with careful silver hair and the face of someone who has always been allowed to have whatever he pointed at.

"Dr. Lennart Voss," he said, and bowed slightly. "Collector. I am told that in your family I am called the Masquerade. I rather like it."

In his other hand lay the compass: the ring of tiny leopards walking nose to tail, the needle like a blade of grass, the word where north should be. Beside it, on the glass of the case, lay a thin sheet of brass with a leopard pressed into its corner, sitting, its tail curled to the left.

"Thirty years," said Voss. "Thirty years I have looked for this. I found the first letter in a dealer's shop in a city of seven hills, sewn into the lining of a leather box. I found this one" (he touched the brass on the glass) "in the base of a plaque that a museum here did not even know it had. Your grandfather and I have been chasing each other round the world for a month. He is very good. He sent me to three cities with riddles on a telephone. Three! I have been to Rome twice this week."

"And the taxi driver," said Isoken. "The soft voice. The seven o'clock news."

He had the grace to look embarrassed. "A small adjustment to a local radio station. A friend of a friend. Your grandfather is a careful man, but he trusts old institutions far too much."

"Where is he?"

"I don't know." Voss sounded almost sorry. "I never had him. I never even saw him. He hid from me far better than the compass did." He looked down at it. "And now I have it, and I find I do not understand it. It does not point north. It points at nothing. I have stood with it in four countries and it simply… trembles."

"It doesn't work for you," said Isoken. "It was never going to."

He held out his open palm, as if to prove her wrong. On it, the compass needle trembled, and then, slowly, as if it had been waiting politely for her to arrive, it turned. Away from him. Towards her. Then past her, towards the high window, towards the south, towards a long way off.

Voss watched it for a long time. When he finally spoke, his soft voice was not amused any more.

"I have bought and sold beautiful things for fifty years," he said, "and not one of them ever wanted to go anywhere." He put the compass in her hand and the brass letter on top of it. "Your grandfather is waiting for you. Go home, Miss Osagie."

The last letter was very short. It said, in the old way of writing that her grandfather had spent his whole life learning to read, exactly what Ehizogie had said to his daughter on the night of the fire, more than a hundred years before.

Epilogue: Home

Benin City · dawn

She walked the last mile at dawn, because some things you should not arrive at in a taxi.

The compass needle never wavered. It pulled her past the roundabout where the museum stands, full of leopards behind glass, and she did not stop. It pulled her past the market, already shouting itself awake, and past the statue of the market woman who, the story goes, once kept a king's secret and saved a kingdom, and she touched the statue's foot for luck and did not stop. It pulled her along the street of the casters, where Pa Eweka, already at his bellows, raised one black hand without looking up, and she did not stop there either.

She stopped where the needle told her to: in front of a long red wall, under the morning, beneath a leopard.

Her grandfather was sitting on the step, eating roasted corn out of a newspaper.

"You're late," he said.

"You left me five voicemails," said Isoken, "and only one of them was mine. You sent me a strip of brass in a code you taught me when I was six. You let a man with a wooden face follow me across three countries. And the taxi driver works for the enemy."

"Ah," said Professor Osagie. "Yes. I was going to mention the taxi driver."

He laughed until he coughed, and she sat down beside him, and for a while neither of them said anything at all.

Then he took her hand, and together they held the compass up towards the wall. The needle swung once, as if it were sighing, and settled on the only word it had ever known.

Home.

THE END
